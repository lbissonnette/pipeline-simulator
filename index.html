    How the model works
    • Time: one tick is 10 seconds. Charts keep the last 12 hours.
    • PDF / CDF: the two distribution charts can show either the share of conversions in each bin (PDF, the histogram) or the cumulative share at or below each value (CDF, rising to 100%). The choice is remembered per chart.
    • Incident markers: every time chart shades the period an incident was active, with a vertical line where it started and where it ended. An × on the end line means it was switched off by hand rather than expiring. Colours follow the incident type: orange for traffic spikes, red for degraded workers and outages, magenta for conversions of death, yellow for upstream holds.
    • Layout: every chart card has a Hide/Show button that collapses it to its title, and a grip (⋮⋮) to drag it above or below other charts. The two in-flight age charts start hidden. The arrangement is remembered in this browser. The parameter panels on the left collapse too; only Inject a delay starts open.
    • Arrivals per tick are Poisson with the mean you set (optionally modulated by a 24-hour sine wave). An upstream delay holds arrivals in a buffer and releases them all at once when it ends.
    • Bundling cuts a bundle whenever the intake buffer holds a full bundle's worth and the dispatcher has room; a partial bundle is flushed after the configured wait. When the dispatcher is full the bundler stops, and conversions accumulate in its buffer.
    • Dispatcher: a bounded FIFO of bundles. Each tick it hands bundles to idle workers. Any idle worker and round robin only use online workers; sticky partition assigns each bundle a worker up front and waits for that worker, even if it is busy or offline, like a static partition assignment. Lowest idle index is first-fit: work concentrates on the lowest-numbered workers and the highest-numbered ones stay idle while there is spare capacity.
    • Processing: each worker holds one bundle. Every tick it draws a rate in conversions per tick from the distribution, multiplies by any degradation, and completes that many conversions. All workers are identical. With the defaults (10 per tick, 1,200 per bundle) the rate-driven part of a bundle takes about 20 minutes. When it is done the worker takes the next bundle on the following tick.
    • Expensive conversions: each conversion is independently expensive with the configured share, so a bundle of B conversions holds Poisson(B × share) of them, scattered at random positions. Each one draws its own cost from a log-normal distribution with the configured mean and spread; when a worker reaches it, it stalls for that cost before continuing. Extra time per bundle is the sum of those costs: mean B × share × cost and standard deviation √(B × share × (cost² + spread²)). Per-tick rate noise averages out over a bundle; this is the mechanism that gives end-to-end times a real spread. No single stall exceeds 4 hours. The defaults (1.8 per bundle at 2 ± 3 min) give a median of about 23 minutes end to end, p90 near 30 minutes and p99 near 45 minutes.
    • Writer: a finished bundle is handed to a buffered writer (256 bundles by default) and written out at the configured rate (9,000 conversions per tick by default, a little above the workers' capacity), in order. Nothing counts as complete until the writer has written the whole bundle, so completeness, fresh time and the latency charts all measure to write-out. When the buffer is full, a worker that finishes a bundle is blocked: it keeps the bundle and takes no new work until a slot frees. The intake policy chooses which blocked worker is admitted first: FIFO (finished earliest), lowest worker index, or random.
    • Capacity = workers × bundle size ÷ expected ticks per bundle, where the expected ticks are the rate-driven part (estimated by simulation) plus the mean stall time. When the load ratio ρ (arrivals ÷ capacity) is above 1 the dispatcher pins at its capacity and the backlog grows without bound at the bundler.
    • Completeness by arrival minute groups conversions by the simulated minute they arrived in and shows, for each minute on the same 12-hour horizon as the other charts, the share already written out. A bundle is written atomically: none of its conversions count until the whole bundle completes, so a minute's completeness stays at 0% for about a bundle time and then jumps. "Complete through" is the most recent minute at which that minute and every older one are at least 99% complete.
    • Age of in-flight data looks at every conversion that has arrived but not yet been written out (held upstream, waiting at the bundler, queued at the dispatcher, or inside a bundle being processed) and shows how old it is now, as a histogram by age and as P50/P90/P99 over time. Ages are grouped by arrival minute. Both histograms size their axis from the widest range needed in the last 12 simulated hours, so the axis grows at once but shrinks only slowly.
    • Where in-flight data is stacks the conversions not yet written out by stage: inside bundles being processed normally (a bundle counts in full until it completes), stuck behind a conversion-of-death stall, sitting on degraded or offline workers, queued at the dispatcher, buffered at the bundler, held upstream by a delay, and at the writer (buffered or finished but blocked on a worker). The top of the stack is the in-flight total; hover for each layer's share.
    • Age percentiles of processed data are the end-to-end ages of bundles at the moment they were written out, over a rolling one-hour window of completions.
    • Fresh time slides a 5-minute window over the arrival minutes and measures each window's completeness (processed ÷ arrived across the five minutes). Scanning from oldest to newest, the P99 fresh time is the age of the oldest window that is still below 99% complete; P90 and P50 use 90% and 50%. Age is counted from the start of the window's oldest minute. It is recomputed every simulated minute and plotted over time; 0 means every window has already passed that threshold.
    • Conversion of Death tags, for its duration, an extra share of arriving conversions as expensive with a fixed cost you choose (still capped at 4 hours). The tag travels with the conversions through the bundler and any upstream hold, so it follows when data arrived rather than when it was bundled. They stack on top of the configured expensive conversions. Workers stalled on one are marked with a red dot; stalls on the configured expensive conversions are not marked.
    • Degraded workers picks a share of the pool, either at random or the lowest-numbered workers, and multiplies their rate for the duration. A multiplier of 0 takes them offline: they stop processing, keep whatever bundle they hold, and idle routing skips them. Any other multiplier slows them while they keep accepting work. Overlapping incidents multiply.
    • Processing time distribution bins the end-to-end time of every bundle completed in the last 12 hours and in the last 7 days, weighted by the conversions in each bundle and normalised to each window's own total, so the two shapes are comparable even though the 7-day set is far larger. Bin width adapts to the widest latency seen.
    • Latency is measured per bundle from the tick its oldest conversion arrived to the tick the bundle finished, so it includes time spent filling the bundle, waiting at the bundler and in the dispatcher, and being processed.
